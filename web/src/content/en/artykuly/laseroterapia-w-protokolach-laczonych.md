*An educational article for physicians. Prepared on the basis of publications presented at the XXIV International Congress of Aesthetic Medicine and Anti-Aging and verified bibliographic data for those works. It does not replace the instructions for use of products and devices or an individual risk assessment.*

## Abstract

The growing popularity of combination therapies in aesthetic medicine requires deliberate planning of treatment order, the depth of action of energy-based devices and the position of the injectable material. The aim of this work is a concise discussion of the available data on combining laser therapy and other energy-based devices (EBD) with botulinum toxin type A (BoNT-A), calcium hydroxyapatite (CaHA), poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA), polycaprolactone (PCL) and cross-linked hyaluronic acid (HA), with particular attention to sequence and time intervals. The review is based on publications identified in the congress materials: Chan et al. (2023), Pomerantz et al. (2021), Semchyshyn and Kilmer (2005), Farkas et al. (2008), Fabi and Goldman (2012), Koren et al. (2019) and de Melo et al. (2020). The data indicate that selected non-ablative lasers, IPL and RF can be performed immediately after BoNT-A injection without a demonstrated loss of toxin efficacy, although the evidence base remains limited. For HA, a histological study in a porcine model showed no significant interaction with 560 nm IPL, 1064 nm Nd:YAG, the 1540 nm fractional laser or superficial ablation, whereas deep fractional Er:YAG 2940 nm and CO2 may reach filler deposits. For CaHA, the best results in treating atrophic scars were obtained when diluted CaHA and the ablative fractional CO2 laser were used in separate sessions. Data for PLLA support combining it with IPL but do not justify automatically transferring these results to deeply ablative lasers. For PCL, recommendations are mainly consensus-based. In clinical practice, the intensity and depth of the EBD should dictate caution: the deeper the thermal or ablative injury and the more superficial the implant, the stronger the case for separating the procedures in time.

*Keywords: laser therapy; aesthetic medicine; botulinum toxin; calcium hydroxyapatite; PLLA; PCL; hyaluronic acid; combination therapy.*


## 1. Introduction

Combination treatment has become one of the basic ways of planning therapy for skin and facial ageing, because individual procedures act on different elements of the ageing phenotype: facial muscle activity, skin quality and pigmentation, the vascular network, the structure of the extracellular matrix and volume deficits. Lasers and IPL make it possible to modulate chromophores and to induce a controlled thermal or ablative response, whereas injectable products act as neuromodulators, volumisers or biostimulators.

The most important practical issue is not whether the procedures can be combined, but when, in what order and at what depth of action. Potential risks include a change in the distribution of botulinum toxin, the effect of the beam or coagulation zone on the implanted material, increased oedema and inflammation, and a cumulative recovery period. On the other hand, appropriately planned treatment can act in a complementary way on different components of the aesthetic problem.

This work is a narrative review. Its aim is to organise information from publications presented in the congress materials provided by the author of the query and to translate it into a practical, conservative scheme for sequencing treatments. The proposed protocols do not replace the instructions for use of a specific device or product, an individual risk assessment or local regulations on registered indications.


## 2. Materials and review method

Publications on combinations of EBD with BoNT-A, HA, PLLA, CaHA and PCL were identified from the photographic materials. Bibliographic data and the main results were verified in PubMed/PMC records or on publishers' websites. Both clinical studies and a histological study in an animal model, as well as a consensus publication, were included in the synthesis. Because of the heterogeneity of indications, devices, parameters, products and endpoints, no meta-analysis was performed. Protocol conclusions are presented as clinical interpretation, not as universal guidelines.


## 3. Botulinum toxin type A and laser therapy


### 3.1. Can a laser inactivate or displace BoNT-A?

Semchyshyn and Kilmer evaluated 19 patients in whom one side of the area injected with BoNT-A was treated within 10 minutes with one of the following devices: PDL/VBeam, 1450 nm laser, 1064 nm Nd:YAG, 560–1200 nm IPL or RF. No reduction in the denervation effect and no visible adverse effects resulting from the immediate use of these non-ablative technologies after the toxin were found [1]. An important limitation is the small sample size, and the results should not be automatically extrapolated to all devices, parameters and injection techniques.

Pomerantz et al. analysed 616 same-day treatments in 393 people, comprising injections into the glabella and/or periorbital area plus a full-face non-ablative fractional 1927 or 1550 nm laser. Eyelid ptosis was noted in one case (0.2%), which indicates that in the studied protocol the risk of clinically detectable diffusion to an unintended muscle was low [2].

The review by Chan et al. also summarises studies in which BoNT-A preceded ablative resurfacing. In the study by Zimbler et al. the toxin was given one week before resurfacing, and in the study by Yamauchi et al. 2–6 weeks before the laser; in both papers combination therapy was associated with greater wrinkle improvement than the laser alone. The authors of the review stress, however, the limited size of the studies and the lack of a clear standard for the order [3].


### 3.2. Proposed practical protocol: BoNT-A + EBD

Based on the data cited, two rational variants can be adopted. For non-ablative technologies with documented safety, the EBD can be performed before BoNT-A or immediately after it, provided the tissues are handled gently. If intensive ablative resurfacing is planned, it is more conservative to separate the procedures and use the sequences described in clinical studies, e.g. BoNT-A about 1–6 weeks before the laser. When the laser is performed first and causes marked erythema, oedema or barrier damage, the injection should be postponed until the tissues have clinically settled.


## 4. Hyaluronic acid and laser therapy


### 4.1. The importance of penetration depth

Key mechanistic data come from the paper by Farkas et al. in a porcine model. Two weeks after three HA products were administered, the injection sites were treated with seven laser/light technologies and then assessed histologically. IPL with a 560 nm filter, Nd:YAG, the 1540 nm fractional laser and superficial ablative ActiveFX showed no contact with, or morphological change to, the filler. In contrast, the deeper ablative fractional systems Er:YAG 2940 nm and DeepFX CO2 created columns of injury reaching the HA deposits [4]. The authors found no unusual tissue damage or clear degradation of the material, but pointed to the possible influence of deep systems on filler longevity or laser efficacy.

The practical implication is anatomical: the risk of interaction depends less on the label “laser + filler” than on the overlap of the thermal/ablative zone with the plane of implantation. The more superficial the HA deposit and the deeper the ablation column, the stronger the case for the sequence “laser first, filler later” or for an appropriate interval between procedures.


### 4.2. Proposed protocol: HA + laser/IPL

For IPL, 1064 nm Nd:YAG and non-ablative fractional lasers with parameters similar to those studied, the available histological data are reassuring, but they come from an animal model. In deeply ablative procedures (particularly fractional Er:YAG 2940 nm and deep CO2), the preferred order is: laser first, then HA after healing and resolution of significant inflammation. If HA has already been administered, its plane and the depth of the planned EBD should be taken into account; superficial deposits in the area of deep ablation argue for postponement or modification of parameters.

## 5. PLLA and light/laser devices

Fabi and Goldman described a retrospective experience of 90 patients in whom IPL was performed immediately before PLLA injection or as early as 6 days after it. In the analysis of 115 IPL exposures (90 respondents), 86.7% of patients reported at least a mild photorejuvenation effect and 64.4% a good or very good correction. After PLLA injections, oedema, bruising, erythema and nodules were observed, among others; importantly, no nodules arising after IPL were recorded in this series [5]. The authors considered the combination of PLLA and IPL safe and effective.

The scope of this work should be interpreted precisely: IPL was studied, not deeply ablative lasers. PLLA is a biostimulator whose effect develops over time following a tissue response. Therefore, in practice the IPL result should not be treated as proof that every type of ablation can be performed shortly after a PLLA injection.


### 5.1. Proposed protocol: PLLA + EBD

If the goal is IPL photorejuvenation, the clinical data support performing IPL before PLLA and, in selected cases, also a short time after the injection. With deeper fractional lasers and a stronger inflammatory response, it is reasonable to separate the procedures until oedema and the acute reaction have resolved. If both methods are planned from the start, the preferred order “EBD → biostimulator” makes it easier to assess the tissues and limits the risk of energy acting directly on freshly deposited material.


## 6. Calcium hydroxyapatite (CaHA) and EBD

Koren et al. analysed 352 patients with atrophic post-acne scars treated with ablative fractional CO2, bipolar RF, the non-ablative 1540 nm fractional laser and diluted CaHA, used alone or in combination. The highest integrated improvement scores were obtained for the combination of diluted CaHA with ablative fractional CO2 performed in separate sessions. The authors also noted more adverse effects and longer downtime in the groups with FACL [6].

The congress material additionally indicates a practical variant of the sequence in which CaHA was given 2–4 weeks before the EBD. However, protocol information from a presentation should be distinguished from the firm conclusion of the publication: the publication shows the advantage of combined treatment in separate sessions but does not establish a single universal interval for all indications, devices and CaHA administration techniques.


### 6.1. Proposed protocol: CaHA + laser

In the treatment of atrophic scars, diluted CaHA and fractional CO2 can be considered as two separate stages. When safety and predictability are the priority, overlapping a deep ablation zone with freshly deposited CaHA should be avoided. If the sequence CaHA before EBD is chosen, an interval of several weeks, consistent with the protocol presented in the congress material, is a more conservative solution than performing both procedures in one session.

## 7. Polycaprolactone (PCL) and EBD

The publication by de Melo et al. is a multidisciplinary consensus on combination therapy with PCL, PLLA/PLGA threads and cross-linked HA, with the possibility of including other procedures, including botulinum toxin and EBD [7]. It is not a study comparing specific lasers with PCL, so the strength of inference about time intervals is lower than in clinical studies.

A congress slide referring to this publication presented a practical rule: EBD 6–8 weeks before PCL or 8–12 weeks after PCL. Such a long interval can be interpreted as a precautionary approach that separates the tissue-response phase after the biostimulator from the thermal injury caused by the device. In a manuscript it is worth labelling this scheme as a consensus/practical recommendation, not as the result of a randomised trial.

## 9. Algorithm for planning combination therapy

1) Define the dominant goal: dynamic wrinkles, photodamage/pigment, erythema/vessels, scars, loss of skin quality or volume deficit.

2) Determine the actual depth of action of the EBD: superficial, non-ablative dermal, or deep fractional ablation.

3) Determine the plane of the injectable product. The risk of interaction increases when the energy zone and the product deposit overlap anatomically.

4) If the evidence concerns exactly the given combination (e.g. IPL + PLLA; selected non-ablative EBD + BoNT-A), the sequence can be based on the studied protocol.

5) If the data are indirect or consensus-based (particularly PCL or deep ablation over a filler/biostimulator), choose a longer interval and perform the procedures in separate sessions.

6) After an ablative procedure, plan the next injectable procedure only after the barrier has been restored and significant erythema, oedema, erosions and signs of infection have resolved.

7) Document the device, wavelength, fluence/energy, pulse duration, density, number of passes, cooling technique, product, dilution, volume and injection plane; without these data the term “laser + filler” is insufficient for a reproducible protocol.


## 10. Discussion

The most important conclusion from the analysed papers is that there is no basis for treating all combination therapies as a single category. The safety of a protocol depends on the physics of the device, the depth of penetration, the way of fractionation, the nature of the injectable material and the time since its administration. Data for BoNT-A are relatively reassuring for several non-ablative technologies, and a large retrospective series with 1550/1927 nm lasers did not show a high frequency of ptosis. At the same time, the literature does not prove that any intensive laser can safely be performed immediately after any toxin injection technique.

The paper by Farkas et al. illustrates well the principle that “depth matters”. Superficial and non-ablative technologies did not come into contact with HA, whereas deep fractional systems could reach the material. This does not automatically mean clinically significant HA degradation, but it justifies caution when planning deep ablation over a fresh or superficially deposited filler.

For biostimulators, the evidence base is uneven. For PLLA there are clinical data on IPL. For CaHA, valuable data concern mainly atrophic scars and show the benefit of combination with fractional CO2 in separate sessions. For PCL, timing schemes are largely based on expert consensus. For this reason, in practice one should avoid presenting all time intervals as equally well proven.

Future studies should report not only the name of the technology but the full energy parameters and injury geometry, the plane and volume of the product, the exact time interval and standardised endpoints. Prospective studies are needed that compare same-day with separated protocols and assess filler longevity and the biostimulatory response after exposure to different EBD depths.


## 11. Limitations

The review is narrative and was guided by the publications visible on the slides provided, so it is not a systematic review of the whole literature. Some data come from retrospective studies, small samples or an animal model. Device parameters and products differ between studies. The terms “laser”, “IPL” and “EBD” are not interchangeable, and a result obtained for one wavelength and one setting should not be directly transferred to other platforms. For PCL, the specific intervals presented in the congress material should be treated as a practical/consensus recommendation.


## 12. Conclusions

1. Selected non-ablative lasers, IPL and RF can be combined with BoNT-A in the same session without a demonstrated loss of toxin efficacy; however, the evidence is device-specific.

2. In deep ablative resurfacing, it is reasonable to separate the procedures; studies on BoNT-A used intervals of about 1–6 weeks.

3. Non-ablative and superficial technologies show little interaction with HA in histological study, whereas deep Er:YAG 2940 nm and fractional CO2 may reach HA deposits.

4. IPL can be combined with PLLA according to published protocols; these data should not be automatically extrapolated to deep ablation.

5. For CaHA, combination with fractional CO2 in separate sessions has clinical support in the treatment of atrophic post-acne scars.

6. For PCL, the intervals between EBD and injection remain primarily a matter of consensus; a more conservative separation of procedures is justified.

7. The most universal principle is to match the order to the EBD depth and the injection plane: the greater their overlap, the greater the need to separate the treatments.
